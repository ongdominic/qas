## Singapore market: customer behaviour insights for QAS

### The broad market backdrop

The Singapore air purifier market sits at roughly USD 207 million in 2025, growing at about 6.3% to USD 281 million by 2030. Residential demand dominates, especially among families and health-conscious individuals, while commercial and healthcare sectors also contribute significantly. One caveat worth knowing internally: air purifiers have historically been perceived as a luxury item, partly owing to high installation and maintenance costs — which is precisely the objection your rental-and-maintained model is built to dissolve. That's a positioning advantage, not just a feature.

---

### AS-150 (purifier) — the trigger is *fear*, the buyer is *health-led*

The dominant emotional driver is haze and health anxiety. PM2.5 is the primary pollutant associated with haze, and inhaling these fine particles has been linked to aggravated asthma, decreased lung function, irregular heartbeat, and respiratory symptoms. Importantly, the worry isn't just seasonal: haze contains PM2.5 that can penetrate deep into the lungs and enter the bloodstream even at moderate PSI levels, and Singapore's everyday air pollution comes primarily from traffic and industry, with haze as a seasonal risk on top.

Behaviourally, this means:
- **Demand spikes are event-driven.** When PSI climbs, GP clinics see an early rise in respiratory consultations and the standard advice is to keep windows closed and run an air purifier. Your enquiry flow should be ready to scale during haze episodes — that's when intent peaks.
- **The anxious segments are specific:** children, pregnant women, the elderly, and those with heart or respiratory conditions are repeatedly singled out in health advisories. This validates your homes/clinics/salons targeting and suggests messaging that names these groups directly converts better than generic "clean air" copy.
- **The competitive frame is HEPA.** HEPA is expected to remain the dominant air purifier segment in Singapore, so your "~30× finer than HEPA" line is doing real work — it's positioning against the category default that buyers already anchor on.

---

### Airsonic (cooler) — the trigger is the *electricity bill*, the buyer is *cost-led*

This is where the numbers are sharpest. Singapore's regulated electricity tariff sits around S$0.29–0.32 per kWh as of 2026, and aircon is the heaviest single load — a single standard unit runs S$20–35+ a month, and cooling a full premises easily reaches S$100–150. At commercial scale this compounds hard, and there's macro pressure too: national power consumption for cooling is projected to grow 66% by 2030.

The behavioural insight: cost-conscious operators of **large, open or semi-open spaces** — factories, warehouses, events — are your audience, because aircon is either unaffordable or physically unviable there. Your "~80% less energy" claim lands against a backdrop where buyers feel the bill monthly. A useful nuance to flag for messaging accuracy: evaporative cooling's efficiency edge is climate-dependent (it works best where it can still add humidity), so positioning it as *spot/area cooling for open industrial spaces* rather than a like-for-like aircon replacement keeps the claim defensible — consistent with your accuracy-over-speed principle.

---

### KOMC (oil mist collector) — the trigger is *compliance and complaints*, the buyer is *risk-led*

This is the strongest regulatory tailwind of the three, and it's not optional spending. NEA requires F&B operators to install an air cleaner such as an electrostatic precipitator to remove grease and smoke from exhaust before discharge, to prevent pollution and nuisance to neighbours. Enforcement is real and rising: in 2026, authorities have tightened scrutiny of exhaust system design, including the use of high-efficiency filters in enclosed or air-conditioned spaces.

Two behavioural levers here:
- **Complaints drive enforcement.** Neighbour complaints about exhaust odour or smoke trigger targeted inspections, and social media content showing non-compliant conditions has prompted enforcement. The pain is reactive — operators act *after* a warning notice or a complaint. Your "complaint-free" angle speaks directly to this.
- **NEA and SFA are separate regimes, and operators conflate them.** A clean SFA food-safety inspection does not protect against NEA non-compliance — an operator with excellent food hygiene can still face demerit points, fines, and suspension from a separate NEA action. There's an education gap here you can own: many F&B operators don't realise their grease/odour discharge is a distinct, separately-enforced liability.

---

### The rental model — why "OPEX not CAPEX" resonates here

Your fully-maintained lease isn't just a pricing choice; it maps onto a documented business preference. Many businesses prefer OpEx to CapEx because operating costs are usually fully deductible in the year incurred, reducing taxable income — leasing equipment rather than buying it allows the full expense deduction in the current period. Beyond tax, moving from a CapEx to an OpEx model shifts the burden of maintenance and upgrades onto the provider rather than the customer — which is *literally* your value proposition ("your air, our responsibility").

The practical takeaway: for SME and F&B buyers especially, frame the lease around **predictable monthly cost, zero maintenance burden, and tax-deductibility** — these are the three levers that turn "air purifier = luxury" into "managed air = sensible operating expense."
